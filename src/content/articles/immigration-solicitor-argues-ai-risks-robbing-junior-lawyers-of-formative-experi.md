# Immigration Solicitor Argues AI Risks Robbing Junior Lawyers of Formative Experience

An immigration solicitor who builds AI tools for lawyers has warned that artificial intelligence risks depriving junior lawyers of the formative experiences they need to develop professional judgement.

Chris Dias, founder of legal AI company Lawyery and a practising immigration solicitor, argues that the real challenge is not the technology itself but how law firms supervise its use. Writing on his company blog, Dias draws on what he describes as a recent Upper Tribunal decision involving AI hallucinations to make the case for structured training frameworks rather than outright bans.

"The issue isn't the hammer, it's who's holding it and whether they know how to use it," Dias writes, responding to what he sees as a pattern of firms either prohibiting AI use entirely or ignoring it altogether.

The argument comes from someone with a foot in both camps. Dias runs an immigration practice whilst building AI tools for legal work, giving him direct experience of both the promise and the pitfalls. His concern centres on junior lawyers who might rely too heavily on AI outputs without developing the underlying legal skills to spot errors or understand context.

Dias points to a case he references as "UK v SSHD [2026] UKUT 81 (IAC)", which he describes as involving AI hallucinations in immigration work. He uses this as evidence that the problem is not AI itself but inadequate supervision of how it is used.

The solution, according to Dias, is for firms to develop structured frameworks for training junior lawyers to work with AI tools. This would involve teaching them both how to use the technology effectively and how to maintain the critical thinking skills that legal work demands.

"We need to get better at teaching juniors how to work with these tools whilst still developing their legal instincts," he argues. "That means proper training, clear protocols, and active supervision. Not wishful thinking that they'll figure it out themselves."

The intervention comes as law firms continue to grapple with AI adoption. Some have implemented comprehensive policies and training programmes, whilst others have taken a more hands-off approach. A few have banned the use of AI tools entirely, though enforcement remains patchy.

Dias's perspective is notable because it comes from someone building the tools he is critiquing. Legal AI companies rarely acknowledge the potential downsides of their products, preferring to focus on efficiency gains and cost savings. His warning about the risk to junior lawyer development suggests a more nuanced view of the technology's impact.

The immigration sector has been particularly active in AI adoption, with tools for case preparation, document analysis, and research becoming increasingly common. The complexity of immigration law, with its frequent changes and procedural requirements, makes it both a promising area for AI assistance and a risky one if errors go unnoticed.

Whether Dias's call for structured training frameworks will be taken up by larger firms remains to be seen. Many are still working out their basic AI policies, let alone comprehensive training programmes for junior staff.
