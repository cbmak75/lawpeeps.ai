A Manhattan federal judge has ruled that conversations with consumer AI chatbots are not protected by attorney-client privilege, ordering a criminal defendant to hand over 31 documents created using Anthropic's Claude AI.

In *United States v. Heppner*, US District Judge Jed S. Rakoff ruled in February that AI-generated materials failed to meet basic privilege requirements. The exchanges were not communications with a lawyer, the user could not reasonably expect confidentiality, and the work was not performed at the direction of counsel.

The case involved Bradley Heppner, former chairman of bankrupt financial services company GWG Holdings, who used Claude to prepare reports about his case. Judge Rakoff's written opinion, issued on 17 February, establishes that simply using AI for legal work does not automatically create privilege protection.

The ruling has prompted immediate policy changes across major law firms. Firms are revising engagement letters and warning clients that anything shared with consumer AI chatbots can be subject to legal discovery. The response suggests firms view this as creating genuine compliance risk rather than affecting only edge cases.

Several firms are now recommending clients use only enterprise-level AI tools with contractual confidentiality guarantees when handling sensitive information. New York firm Debevoise & Plimpton has updated its guidance to suggest that AI legal research is more likely to receive privilege protection when conducted at a lawyer's direction.

The firm recommends users include specific language in prompts: "I am doing this research at the direction of counsel for X litigation."

This creates a practical problem for smaller firms and solo practitioners who may not have access to enterprise AI tools with the same contractual protections. The ruling effectively creates a two-tier system where larger firms with enterprise contracts can use AI more safely than smaller practices relying on consumer tools.

The decision also raises questions about work product protection for AI-assisted research and document preparation. If consumer AI platforms cannot maintain confidentiality, lawyers may need to fundamentally rethink how they integrate these tools into their practice.

The industry response has been swift. Law firms are scrambling to update internal policies and client contracts to mitigate exposure. Some are implementing blanket restrictions on consumer AI use for client matters, while others are developing detailed protocols for when and how AI can be safely deployed.

*This ruling creates the first clear judicial precedent on AI privilege protection in the US. The speed of law firm policy updates suggests the industry recognises this as establishing genuine compliance risk, not just affecting unusual cases. The practical impact may be to accelerate adoption of enterprise AI tools among larger firms while creating barriers for smaller practices. – mm!ke*
