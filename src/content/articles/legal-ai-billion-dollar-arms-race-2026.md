Legora's announcement of a $550 million Series D at a $5.55 billion valuation marks more than just another funding milestone. Combined with Harvey's $200 million raise at an $11 billion valuation earlier this month, these two companies now command more than $16 billion in enterprise value—nearly three times the size of the entire legal AI software market according to Research and Markets.

This is not sustainable competition. It is the early stages of market consolidation, and it poses genuine risks for the legal profession's technology future.

The Stockholm-based startup tripled its valuation in just five months, rising from $1.8 billion in October 2025 to its current $5.55 billion. Accel led the round with participation from Benchmark, Bessemer Venture Partners, General Catalyst, ICONIQ Growth, Redpoint Ventures, and Y Combinator—a who's who of Silicon Valley capital.

Legora has grown from 40 to 400 employees globally and now serves 800+ customers across 50+ markets with tens of thousands of daily users. The funding will fuel US expansion, with new offices planned for Houston and Chicago and a target of 300+ US employees by the end of 2026.

These numbers tell a story of genuine product-market fit. But they also tell a story about capital concentration that should concern anyone who cares about competitive legal technology markets.

When two companies attract this level of venture funding, they operate by different rules than their competitors. They can subsidise pricing below sustainable levels. They can outspend rivals on talent acquisition. They can afford to integrate with every major legal software platform while smaller competitors must choose their battles carefully.

This creates a two-tiered market. Well-funded platforms like Legora and Harvey can offer comprehensive solutions at artificially low prices, making it difficult for specialised tools to compete on individual features. Traditional legal software vendors, many of which lack venture backing, face disruption from companies that can afford to lose money while building market share.

The real risk is not to Magic Circle firms or AmLaw 200 practices. They will have their choice of well-funded platforms competing for their business. The risk is to the rest of the legal profession: sole practitioners, small firms, barristers' chambers, and regional practices that may find themselves priced out of competitive technology or locked into platforms that prioritise enterprise customers.

Legora's geographic expansion from Europe to the US follows a pattern we are seeing across legal AI. European companies with strong regulatory compliance capabilities are using venture funding to enter the American market, while US companies expand globally from positions of capital strength. This is creating a homogenised competitive landscape dominated by venture-backed platforms rather than diverse, specialised tools.

The legal profession has historically benefited from a competitive ecosystem of software vendors serving different practice areas, firm sizes, and jurisdictions. Consolidation around a handful of well-funded platforms threatens that diversity.

I am not suggesting that Legora or Harvey are bad actors. Both companies appear to be building genuinely useful products and serving their customers well. The problem is structural: when venture capital creates artificial pricing competition, it distorts markets in ways that benefit well-funded incumbents and harm long-term competition.

The legal profession should be paying attention to this funding concentration, not celebrating it. Access to competitive legal technology should not depend on whether a company can raise hundreds of millions in venture capital.
