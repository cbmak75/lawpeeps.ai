The Ministry of Justice is testing whether its in-house AI transcription system can match commercial providers on accuracy while cutting costs that currently see victims paying thousands of pounds for court records.

The study of "Justice Transcribe" represents a notable shift toward government building AI capabilities internally rather than procuring them from the market. HM Courts & Tribunals Service will benchmark the system against established commercial transcription vendors, with findings feeding what the MoJ calls "nationwide plans to upgrade, modernise and open up the court system."

The immediate policy driver is access to justice. Transcript fees currently reach thousands of pounds for victims seeking court records. The government has committed to providing free transcripts of judges' sentencing remarks to Crown Court victims from Spring 2027, creating pressure to find a cost-effective solution at scale.

But Justice Transcribe sits within a broader pattern of central government departments developing AI tools in-house rather than buying them off the shelf. The Department for Transport's Consultation Analysis Tool follows similar logic: build internal capability that can be tailored to government-specific requirements and data handling constraints.

This approach puts government AI development in direct competition with commercial legal tech vendors who have built businesses around court transcription services. The MoJ's decision to benchmark Justice Transcribe against commercial providers suggests it is treating this as a genuine build-versus-buy decision rather than a foregone conclusion.

Family law specialists have welcomed the access-to-justice goal while raising technical concerns about implementation. They argue that anonymisation alone will not prevent children being identified through contextual detail in family court proceedings. AI transcription cannot simply inherit safeguards designed for paper-and-human-transcriber processes.

The Law Society's Vice-president Brett Dixon has called for audio recordings to be retained so accuracy can be independently verified. This maps directly onto bias-mitigation approaches used by other government AI tools, where auditability becomes a design requirement rather than an afterthought.

The technical standards question is central to the pilot's commercial implications. If Justice Transcribe can meet accuracy requirements at significantly lower cost than commercial vendors, other government departments may follow suit with their own in-house AI development. If it cannot, the pilot becomes evidence for the continued value of commercial procurement.

The study timeline and methodology have not been disclosed. The MoJ did not respond to questions about whether commercial vendors will have access to the same test data sets or evaluation criteria used to assess Justice Transcribe.

What is clear is that this pilot represents more than cost-saving automation. It is a test case for whether government can develop competitive AI capabilities internally, with implications that extend well beyond court transcription.
