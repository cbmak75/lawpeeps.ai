A federal judge in New York has ruled that communications with public AI tools are not protected by attorney-client privilege, creating the first nationwide precedent on AI privilege protection and establishing a framework that distinguishes between different types of AI platforms.

In *United States v. Heppner*, Judge Jed S. Rakoff of the Southern District of New York ruled on 17 February that a criminal defendant's exchanges with Anthropic's Claude during securities fraud proceedings were not protected by privilege or work product doctrine. The case involved 31 AI-generated documents that the defendant claimed were privileged.

Judge Rakoff described the issue as "a question of first impression nationwide" and applied the traditional three-part test for attorney-client privilege. The court found that public AI tools fail all three requirements: the AI is not an attorney, the platform's privacy policy disclaims confidentiality, and the defendant used the tool independently without counsel's direction.

The ruling emphasises that privilege protections depend on "a trusting human relationship" involving fiduciary duties and professional discipline—features absent from publicly available AI platforms. "Claude is not an attorney," Judge Rakoff noted, highlighting the fundamental structural differences between AI tools and human legal counsel.

## Enterprise tools may fare differently

The decision creates an important distinction that could reshape how law firms approach AI adoption. Enterprise AI tiers with contractual confidentiality protections and attorney oversight may receive different treatment from courts, though no federal court has directly ruled on this question.

The reasoning in *Heppner* suggests that three factors could prove decisive for enterprise AI privilege protection: contractual confidentiality guarantees, direct attorney supervision of AI use, and vendor structures that create professional obligations rather than consumer relationships.

Legal analysts have noted that two recent cases involving self-represented litigants reached different conclusions, with courts finding that privilege was not waived despite the use of public AI tools. However, those cases involved different procedural contexts and did not directly address the initial creation of privilege.

## Practical implications

The ruling creates immediate practical questions for law firms already using AI tools. Public platforms like Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini now carry documented privilege risks that firms must weigh against their utility for legal work.

The decision does not create an AI exception to privilege law, but rather applies existing privilege doctrine to AI communications. This means that properly structured enterprise AI implementations could still qualify for protection if they meet traditional privilege requirements.

Law firms will need to evaluate their AI workflows against the three-part test: whether the AI operates under attorney supervision, whether confidentiality is contractually guaranteed, and whether the relationship creates the professional obligations that underpin privilege protection.

The ruling also highlights the importance of understanding AI vendor terms of service. Public AI platforms typically include broad data use permissions that are incompatible with confidentiality requirements, whilst enterprise contracts may offer stronger protection.

This precedent arrives as law firms are rapidly expanding AI adoption across practice areas. The distinction between public and enterprise AI tools now carries direct privilege implications that could influence procurement decisions and risk management strategies.
