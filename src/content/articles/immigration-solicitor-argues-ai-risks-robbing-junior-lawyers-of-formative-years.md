An immigration solicitor who builds AI tools for lawyers has warned that artificial intelligence risks depriving junior practitioners of the formative experiences that build legal judgement, but argues the solution lies in better supervision rather than technology bans.

Chris Dias, founder of legal AI startup Lawyery and principal at Dias Solicitors, draws on what he describes as the recent Upper Tribunal decision in UK v SSHD [2026] UKUT 81 (IAC) to make his case. According to Dias, this decision involved AI hallucinations in immigration work, though I could not independently verify the details of this tribunal ruling.

Writing on his company blog, Dias argues that firms are approaching AI adoption with junior lawyers in the wrong way. "The real issue isn't the technology itself, it's that we're not thinking about how juniors develop legal reasoning when AI does the heavy lifting," he writes.

Dias suggests that traditional legal training relies on juniors working through routine tasks that build pattern recognition and legal intuition. When AI handles document drafting, research, and initial case analysis, he argues, juniors miss these developmental stages but still need to supervise AI output they may not fully understand.

"You can't quality-check work in areas where you haven't built competence," Dias writes. "But that's exactly what we're asking junior lawyers to do when we hand them AI tools without proper frameworks."

His solution involves what he calls "structured AI training frameworks" that deliberately expose junior lawyers to the underlying work that AI automates. Rather than banning AI tools or allowing unrestricted use, Dias suggests firms should require juniors to work through cases manually before using AI assistance, then compare outputs.

The immigration practitioner's perspective carries particular weight given his dual role as both AI developer and practicing solicitor. Lawyery builds AI tools specifically for immigration law, putting Dias in the position of creating technology he then argues needs careful implementation.

Dias acknowledges this apparent contradiction but frames it as evidence that the technology itself is not the problem. "I build these tools because they genuinely help lawyers serve clients better," he writes. "But I also see how they can undermine junior development if we're not thoughtful about implementation."

The piece reflects broader tensions in legal education as AI tools become more sophisticated. While senior lawyers debate whether to embrace or restrict AI, junior practitioners often find themselves caught between expectations to use new technology and traditional learning methods that assume manual work.

Immigration law presents a particularly interesting case study for AI adoption. The field involves high volumes of routine documentation alongside complex case law, making it both well-suited to AI assistance and dependent on human judgement about individual circumstances.

Dias's arguments echo concerns raised by legal educators about AI's impact on skill development, but his focus on supervision frameworks rather than technology restrictions offers a more nuanced approach than outright bans.

The blog post suggests Dias sees AI adoption as inevitable but argues the legal profession needs better structures to manage the transition. His call for "wilful intelligence rather than wilful blindness" about AI's impact on junior development may resonate with firms struggling to balance efficiency gains with training responsibilities.
