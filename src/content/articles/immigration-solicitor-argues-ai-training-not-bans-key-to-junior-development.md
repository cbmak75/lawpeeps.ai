# Immigration Solicitor Argues AI Training, Not Bans, Key to Junior Development

An immigration solicitor who also builds legal AI tools has published a pointed critique of how law firms handle artificial intelligence training for junior lawyers, arguing that blanket bans risk creating a generation unprepared for modern practice.

Chris Dias, founder of both Dias Solicitors and legal tech startup Lawyery, draws on a recent Upper Tribunal decision to make the case that supervision and structured training matter more than the tools themselves. His argument cuts against the grain of firms that have responded to AI concerns by restricting access rather than teaching proper use.

Writing on the Lawyery blog, Dias references the Upper Tribunal decision in UK v SSHD [2026] UKUT 81 (IAC), which he describes as "the Hamid AI hallucinations case". The decision apparently involved issues with AI-generated content in legal submissions, though the specific details remain unclear from publicly available sources.

Dias argues that such cases demonstrate the real problem: inadequate supervision rather than inherent flaws in AI tools. "The issue isn't the technology," he writes. "It's that we're not teaching people how to use it responsibly."

His proposed solution involves structured AI training frameworks for junior lawyers. Rather than prohibition or what he calls "wilful blindness", firms should develop systematic approaches to teaching appropriate AI use, verification methods, and the limits of current technology.

The argument comes from someone with a foot in both camps. Dias runs an immigration practice while building AI tools for lawyers, giving him insight into both the practical challenges of legal work and the technical realities of what AI can and cannot do.

His timing is notable. As more firms grapple with AI policies, many have defaulted to restrictive approaches. Some ban AI use entirely. Others permit it only for senior lawyers. Few have invested in comprehensive training programmes.

Dias suggests this approach creates a dangerous skills gap. Junior lawyers trained without AI exposure will eventually inherit practices where such tools are standard. The alternative—learning on the job without proper foundations—risks the kind of problems highlighted in the tribunal case he cites.

The immigration context adds weight to his argument. Immigration law often involves high-stakes work with tight deadlines and vulnerable clients. If AI tools can be used safely in such settings, the training approach matters enormously.

Dias's dual perspective as practitioner and founder gives his analysis particular credibility. Unlike commentators who theorise about AI from the sidelines, he deals daily with both the promise and limitations of these tools.

Whether his proposed training frameworks will gain traction remains to be seen. Structured AI education requires investment and admitting that current approaches may be inadequate. For firms already struggling with AI policy, that represents another challenge rather than a simple solution.
